# Ho Chi Minh City: History, Street Food and the Case for Walking

Saigon does not ease you in. Two minutes after leaving the airport you are inside a river of motorbikes, and the city keeps that pace until roughly 2 am. Most visitors try to tame it with a checklist — museum, market, rooftop, tunnels — and end up exhausted and oddly unmoved.

The city rewards a different approach: fewer stops, longer meals, and a willingness to walk one neighbourhood properly instead of taxiing between five. This Ho Chi Minh City travel guide covers what is worth your time, what it costs, and which stories about Saigon are simply not true.

## Why Saigon resists a checklist

**The history is not behind glass.** The War Remnants Museum presents the American war from the Vietnamese side, with a directness that unsettles visitors from every country. Two hours there will do more than a week of plaques.

**The old city is walkable.** District 1 holds the cathedral, the post office, the opera house, the palace and the riverside within about 2 km. Dong Khoi Street alone is an architecture lesson from the 1880s to the 2020s.

**Eating happens on the street, and it happens early.** The best bánh mì is gone by mid-morning; the best cơm tấm is a breakfast dish. Saigon's food clock runs four hours ahead of a European one.

**The city has two centres of gravity.** District 1 is the postcard. District 3, District 5 and Phú Nhuận are where people actually live and eat. You need both.

## When to go

| Season | Months | Conditions | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dry | Dec–Apr | 24–33°C, lower humidity, blue skies | Best window, especially Jan–Mar |
| Hot | Mar–Apr | 33–37°C, sticky before the rains break | Do mornings and evenings |
| Wet | May–Nov | Short, heavy afternoon downpours, 30–60 minutes | Fewer crowds, lower prices, lush streets |

The rain is more manageable than its reputation: it arrives, it dumps, it stops, and the city dries within twenty minutes. Carry a light poncho rather than an umbrella for the motorbike traffic.

**Tết** (late January or February) empties the city of many small businesses for three to five days while filling it with returning families. It is atmospheric and inconvenient in equal measure.

For a three-day trip, District 1 or the edge of District 3 is the right call. You will walk to most of what you came for.

## Three days that do not collapse

### Day 1 — War, faith and colonial bones

Start at the **War Remnants Museum** (28 Võ Văn Tân, open daily 7:30–17:30; adult admission 60,000 VND as of October 2026). The courtyard holds tanks and aircraft; the indoor floors hold the photographs, including the Agent Orange gallery and the Requiem collection. Expect to be quiet afterwards.

Walk to the **Reunification Palace**, the 1960s building whose gates a North Vietnamese tank pushed open on 30 April 1975. The war rooms and the rooftop helicopter are preserved exactly as they were left, which is the point.

Afternoon: the **Central Post Office** and **Notre Dame Cathedral** face each other across Paris Commune Square. Then **Book Street** (Nguyễn Văn Bình) for coffee under the trees.

Evening: a rooftop. The Bitexco tower and the riverside hotels all sell the same skyline from different heights — pick one, go up before sunset, and stay for the twenty minutes when the city switches its lights on.

### Day 2 — Củ Chi, then Chinatown

The **Củ Chi tunnels** are 1.5–2 hours northwest of the centre. Entry is 110,000 VND at Bến Đình and 90,000 VND at Bến Dược, open 7:00–17:00; the tunnel network originally ran for some 250 km, and a short section has been widened for visitors to crawl through. Go early to avoid both the heat and the tour buses. Bến Dược is the larger, quieter site and includes a memorial temple.

Afternoon: **Chợ Lớn** — the Thiên Hậu temple with its incense spirals, and Bình Tây market, which is a wholesale market rather than a souvenir one. If you have energy left, the **FITO museum** of traditional medicine is small, well curated and almost empty.

Evening: **Bến Thành market** night stalls from around 6 pm, then **Bùi Viện** if you want the noise. Both are touristy. Both are also genuinely fun for one night.

### Day 3 — choose one

- **Food crawl, Districts 1–3–Phú Nhuận:** bánh mì Huỳnh Hoa, cơm tấm Ba Ghiền, bún mọc Cô Ba, chè. Start at 7 am and eat small portions.
- **Mekong Delta, Mỹ Tho or Bến Tre:** about 2 hours by road, coconut canals, fruit orchards, honey tea. Long day, low effort, good with kids.
- **Củ Chi + Tây Ninh:** combine the tunnels with the Cao Đài Holy See, whose noon ceremony is worth timing your day around.
- **Slow Saigon:** Jade Emperor Pagoda, Tân Định pink church, the back lanes of District 3, an afternoon in a cafe that roasts its own beans.

Two rules. **Eat breakfast like a local** — cơm tấm and hủ tiếu are morning food, and the shops that do them well close by early afternoon. **Follow the queue, not the signboard** — the bánh mì shop with fifteen people waiting is not a marketing strategy.

## The two colonial landmarks, and one myth

**Notre Dame Cathedral** was built between 1877 and 1880 with red bricks shipped from Marseille, and it is still the city's most recognisable silhouette. It has been under restoration for years, with completion now expected around 2027, so the interior is closed to sightseeing; Masses still take place. The best view is from the small park opposite, where the statue of the Virgin Mary stands.

**The Central Post Office** next door was built between 1886 and 1891 and designed by **Marie-Alfred Foulhoux**, the architect-in-chief of Cochinchina — not by Gustave Eiffel, despite what nearly every guidebook and taxi driver will tell you. The Eiffel story is a persistent myth that French architectural records and the 1891 inauguration report disprove. It is still a beautiful building, with vaulted ceilings, old maps and wooden phone booths, and you can genuinely post a letter from inside.

## Practical details

**Airport to the centre.** Bus **109** (yellow, air-conditioned) runs to the Bến Thành area for about 15,000 VND, roughly 5:30 am to 11 pm, taking 45 minutes. Note that since April 2025 it serves the new domestic Terminal 3, so international arrivals may need the free terminal shuttle first. Bus **152** is cheaper (5,000–6,000 VND) but slower and crowded. Grab or Be runs 110,000–250,000 VND; use the app rather than an unmetered taxi.

**Crossing the road.** Walk steadily, do not sprint, do not stop. Motorbikes flow around predictable movement. Raise a hand if you want to signal intent.

**Money.** Cash for markets and street food; cards in hotels and malls. Watch for the occasional "no change" trick on large notes.

**Motorbike taxis.** Grab Bike is the fastest way through traffic and costs a fraction of a car. Wear the helmet they provide; it is the law and it is enforced.

**Heat.** Between 11 am and 3 pm, do indoor things. Museums and malls are air-conditioned for a reason.

**Bùi Viện.** Fun for an hour, expensive by local standards, and the venue for most of the city's drink scams. Agree prices before ordering, keep your phone in a front pocket.

## FAQ

### How many days do I need in Ho Chi Minh City?
Three days covers the city and one day trip comfortably. Two days is enough for the centre if you skip Củ Chi; five days lets you add the Mekong and actually relax.

### Is the War Remnants Museum suitable for children?
The courtyard exhibits are fine for most ages, but several indoor galleries contain graphic images of injury and death. Use judgement, and consider splitting the visit.

### Notre Dame Cathedral — can I go inside?
Not for sightseeing while restoration continues (currently expected to finish around 2027). You can attend Mass, and the exterior is fully visible.

### Củ Chi or the Mekong Delta?
Củ Chi if you want history and can handle a hot, cramped half-day. The Mekong if you want landscape and a gentler pace. Doing both in three days makes for two long days in a row.

### How do I get around?
Grab and Be for most trips, walking for District 1, and the metro line for the eastern corridor. Rental bicycles are not practical in District 1 traffic.
