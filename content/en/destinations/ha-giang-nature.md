# Ha Giang beyond the loop: markets, homestays and the people who actually live there

Most visitors treat Ha Giang as a road. They ride the loop, stop at the viewpoints, take the photographs, and leave with a strong impression of limestone and almost none of the people who farm it.

That is a shame, because the province is one of the most culturally layered places in Vietnam — more than twenty ethnic groups, each with its own language, dress and calendar, living in valleys that are a two-hour drive apart and feel much further. This guide to Ha Giang culture follows the markets, the homestays and the seasonal work that shape the plateau.

This guide is about the other half of the trip: the markets, the homestays, the seasonal landscapes, and the etiquette that determines whether your presence is welcome.

> **Administrative note (2026):** since 1 July 2025 Ha Giang has been part of **Tuyen Quang province**. The place names travellers use — Dong Van, Meo Vac, Yen Minh, Quan Ba, Hoang Su Phi — are unchanged, and locals still say "Ha Giang" for the whole region.

## Why the culture here is different

Ha Giang sits against the Chinese border, in a landscape of limestone peaks that isolated valleys from each other for centuries. That isolation preserved distinct languages, textiles and rituals that elsewhere in Vietnam have blurred into a general lowland culture.

The result is a province where a market is not an attraction but an economy: livestock traded live, indigo cloth sold by the metre, herbal medicine weighed on handheld scales, and corn wine poured before nine in the morning.

## The main groups, and what distinguishes them

| Group | What you will notice | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Hmong** (several sub-groups) | Indigo-dyed hemp, silver jewellery, steep-slope farming; sub-groups distinguished by headscarf and embroidery | Dong Van, Meo Vac, Quan Ba |
| **Tay** | Stilt houses with palm-thatch roofs, wet-rice farming in valley floors, then singing duets | Yen Minh, Bac Me, Thon Tha |
| **Dao** (Red Dao, Coin Dao) | Red turbans and shaved brows among Red Dao; heavy silver coin necklaces among Coin Dao; herbal medicine passed down through women | Sa Phin, Lung Cu areas |
| **Nung** | Indigo clothing, paper-making | Northern valleys |
| **Giay** | Colourful headscarves, skilled wet-rice farmers, traditional music after dinner | Meo Vac |
| **Lo Lo** | Bronze drums, ancestor worship | Lung Cu, Dong Van |
| **Pu Peo** | One of Vietnam's smallest groups, fewer than a thousand people | Northern border communes |

A practical note: "Hmong" is an umbrella term. Wearing the wrong sub-group's pattern is not a compliment — buy textiles from the people who made them.

## Markets: the backbone of the week

Markets run on fixed days, and getting the day right is the single biggest planning decision in a cultural trip. Most start early — livestock trading happens between 7 and 9 am — and wind down by late morning.

| Market | Day | What it is good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Dong Van old town** | Daily, biggest at weekends | Clay-brick lanes, mixed traders, easy access |
| **Meo Vac** | Sunday | The most photogenic: full ceremonial dress, a separate livestock area, a textile alley |
| **Pho Cao** | Sunday | Hmong, Tay and Giay traders; live buffalo and pig trade; horse-meat stew from street stalls |
| **Lung Tam** | Daily except Sunday | A Hmong women's weaving cooperative; backstrap looms, hemp, natural indigo |

Two rules that keep markets worth visiting:

1. **Ask before photographing people**, especially elders and anyone in ceremonial dress. A gesture and a smile works; a long lens pointed at someone's face does not.
2. **Buy something.** A metre of indigo cloth or a small embroidered piece costs little and means the market remains a market rather than a stage.

## Homestays: sleeping in a house, not a hotel

The homestay model here is genuinely domestic: you sleep in a family's stilt house, eat what the family cooked, and share the space on their terms.

| Base | Character | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| **Thon Tha (Tay)** | Valley-floor stilt houses near Ha Giang city | Family meals, bamboo raft building on the stream, easy first night |
| **Dong Van old quarter** | Clay-brick house with a family altar | Corn wine ceremony at dusk, walkable to the old town |
| **Meo Vac (Giay)** | Traditional music after dinner — khen, tinh tau | Rice wine rituals; refusing outright is impolite, sipping is not |
| **Lung Cu (Lo Lo)** | Border village, very basic | Flag tower views, occasional bronze drum ceremonies |

**Etiquette that matters more than tips:**

- Take your shoes off at the stairs.
- Do not step on the sleeping-mat area.
- Ask before entering a kitchen.
- Do not hand sweets or money to children without asking their parents.
- If you are offered rice wine, sip. Refusing the gesture entirely causes more offence than drinking badly.
- A shared cup is normal. If you have health concerns, say so early and kindly rather than at the third round.

## Landscapes worth timing your trip around

**Rice terraces, September to October.** Hoang Su Phi, Xin Man and the Quang Binh district produce the classic stair-step photographs. Best light is 6–8 am and 4–6 pm; midday flattens everything.

**Buckwheat flowers, October to December.** White and pink fields across the Dong Van and Meo Vac valleys, with a festival in November. The bloom is short and varies by year — ask locally rather than trusting a fixed date.

**Nho Que river.** The turquoise ribbon in the gorge below Ma Pi Leng. The colour is strongest between about 10 am and 2 pm, when the sun is high enough to reach the water.

**Quan Ba Heaven's Gate.** The first pass on the loop, with the twin hills below. Go early; by mid-morning the tour groups arrive.

**Dong Van Karst Plateau.** 2,356 km² of limestone recognised by UNESCO in 2010 — Vietnam's first global geopark. The fossils embedded in the rock date back hundreds of millions of years.

## Experiences that are participatory rather than observed

- **A weaving session at Lung Tam:** backstrap loom, natural dyes from indigo, turmeric and bark. Half a day, and you keep what you make.
- **Breakfast with a family in Dong Van:** steamed rice sheets rolled with herbs and local sausage, made in front of you.
- **Corn wine at dusk:** a shared cup and a toast to the ancestors. Short, formal, and easy to get wrong by rushing.
- **The Vuong palace at Sa Phin:** a century-old mansion blending local and European architecture, built on opium trade wealth. The history is uncomfortable and worth hearing properly.
- **Evening music:** ask your host about khen (bamboo mouth organ) or tinh tau (lute). It is often offered if you show interest, and rarely offered unprompted.

## Travelling without doing damage

1. **Ask before photographing.** Especially elders, ceremonies and children.
2. **Buy direct from makers.** Middlemen take a large cut; a purchase at the cooperative or the house pays the person who did the work.
3. **Treat sacred sites as sacred.** Family altars, spirit trees and burial grounds are not backdrops. No touching, no raised voices, no selfies.
4. **Learn three phrases.** "Chao" (hello), "cam on" (thank you), "khoe khong" (how are you). An attempt at Vietnamese goes a long way; an attempt at a local language goes further and will usually produce laughter.
5. **Do not distribute sweets or pens to children.** It teaches a transaction where there should be none and pulls children out of school hours.
6. **Carry your rubbish out.** Some villages have no collection at all.

## Building a route that includes culture, not just corners

A workable five-day structure that keeps the riding manageable:

| Day | Plan |
|---|---|
| **1** | Ha Giang city → Quan Ba → Yen Minh, night in a Tay stilt house at Thon Tha or Yen Minh |
| **2** | Yen Minh → Lung Cu → Sa Phin → Dong Van, night in the old quarter |
| **3** | Dong Van → Ma Pi Leng → Meo Vac (market if Sunday), night in a Giay homestay |
| **4** | Meo Vac → Bao Lam → Ha Giang city, or extend toward Hoang Su Phi |
| **5** | Hoang Su Phi terraces at dawn, then back to Ha Giang |

If markets are your priority, work backwards from the Sunday calendar: Meo Vac and Pho Cao both run on Sunday, and they are far enough apart that you can only do one properly.

## FAQ

**How many ethnic groups live in Ha Giang?**
Sources commonly cite more than twenty, with the Hmong, Tay, Dao, Nung, Giay, Lo Lo and Pu Peo among the most visible to visitors. Exact counts vary between official statistics and local usage.

**Can I visit a market without a guide?**
Yes, but you need transport and the right day. A local guide is worth it for context and for introductions.

**Are homestays suitable for families?**
Yes, with expectations set. Facilities are basic, bathrooms are often shared, and the experience is the point.

**Is it appropriate to give gifts to host families?**
Small, useful things are welcome — fruit, coffee, school supplies if you have asked first. Cash gifts create awkwardness; paying fairly for meals and purchases is better.

**When are the buckwheat flowers out?**
Typically late October to early December, with a November festival. The exact peak shifts each year.

**Do I need a permit for the border areas?**
Some sections require one, usually handled by your operator. Carry your passport, not a photocopy.
