# Hoi An Food Guide: What to Eat, and Where Locals Actually Eat It

Hoi An was a trading port for four centuries, and its kitchen still shows the paperwork: Chinese noodle technique, Japanese patience, French bread, Cham-era wells, and a local obsession with herbs grown three kilometres away. The result is a food culture that is easy to photograph and surprisingly hard to understand in a single evening.

This guide is organised around what matters — which dishes are genuinely local, what they should cost, which addresses are reliable, and how to eat without spending your trip in a queue.

## Why Hoi An eats differently

Three things set the town apart from the rest of central Vietnam.

**Water.** Hoi An sits on the Thu Bồn River, on a layer of wells dug centuries ago. One of them, the Bà Lễ well, is usually dated to the 10th century Cham period. Whether or not minerals in that water truly create the texture of the local noodles, cooks here believe it, and the belief has shaped a dish you cannot get anywhere else.

**Herbs, not garnish.** Trà Quê village, about 3 km north, grows the mint, perilla, fish leaf and Vietnamese coriander that show up on every table. The herb plate is not decoration; it is half the dish.

**Scale.** This is a small city that receives millions of visitors a year. That creates both a superb restaurant scene and a large number of places selling the same six dishes badly. Position matters more than stars.

## Four dishes that need explaining

### Cao lầu

The one dish you cannot eat anywhere else. The noodles are thicker than phở, slightly smoky, and made with water and a lye solution traditionally derived from wood ash. On top: slices of char siu-style pork, a shard of crisp rice cracker, fresh herbs, and just enough sauce to coat rather than drown.

The legend — repeated by every cook in town — is that the water must come from the Bà Lễ well near Trần Hưng Đạo street. The well is real, roughly 1,100 years old, and free to look at. Whether the mineral content is the actual difference or the technique is, the result is unmistakable: firm, almost al dente, with a faintly alkaline finish that pairs with the sweet pork.

Eat it at lunch, at a stall with plastic stools and a short menu. If a restaurant offers twenty dishes, the cao lầu is probably not the reason anyone is there.

### White rose dumplings

*Bánh bao* and *bánh vạc*: rice-flour wrappers pressed paper-thin, pleated into something like a rose, filled with seasoned shrimp, steamed, then finished with fried shallots and a sweet-sour fish sauce. The dish arrived with Hoi An's Chinese community, but the pleating became local.

Almost every white rose dumpling sold in town is made by a small number of Hoa families who keep the dough ratio in the family — the best known is the White Rose Restaurant at 533 Hai Bà Trưng, where you can watch the wrappers being made from the dining room. A plate is 60,000–90,000 VND; go before noon, when the workshop is active and the plates are freshest.

One detail most visitors miss: a proper plate is mixed — several shrimp dumplings and a few of the vegetable-filled ones. If you are served only one kind, ask.

### Bánh mì

Hoi An's version is a full meal in a baguette: pâté, cold cuts or grilled pork, pickled daikon and carrot, cucumber, coriander, chilli, and a house sauce that varies by family.

**Bánh Mì Phượng** (2B Phan Chu Trinh) became famous after Anthony Bourdain filmed there in 2009 and called it "a symphony in a sandwich." The queue at peak hours runs 20–30 minutes, the bread is genuinely good, and a sandwich costs 30,000–45,000 VND. Go at 7–8 am, before the coach groups.

**Madam Khanh** (115 Trần Cao Vân), known as the Bánh Mì Queen, has been feeding locals for four decades, with a shorter wait and a grilled-pork version many people prefer. Both are good; they are simply different sandwiches.

Ignore the claim that a US president ate at Phượng. He did not. That story belongs to a bún chả shop in Hanoi.

### Cơm gà

Hoi An's chicken rice borrows the Hainanese idea and changes everything: turmeric-tinted rice, hand-shredded poached chicken, a herb salad with banana blossom, and a small bowl of chicken broth on the side. Mix it, add the chilli sauce, and use the broth to soften the rice as you go.

Two long-standing addresses: **Cơm Gà Bà Buội** (22 Phan Chu Trinh) and **Cơm Gà Xí** (47/2 Trần Hưng Đạo). Both are lunch places; both sell out.

## Where locals eat

**Central Market, 5–7 am.** The most honest food in town. Bánh cuốn steamed to order, cao lầu for breakfast, herbs at their freshest, vendors not yet tired.

**Back lanes off the Old Town.** Walk one street behind the lantern-facing facades and prices drop by a third. Follow the smoke at dawn.

**Trà Quê village, 3 km.** Bicycle out in the morning, harvest herbs, eat a farm lunch. Several families run cooking classes here.

**Cẩm Nam Island, 5 minutes across the bridge.** Family yards become open-air grills at dusk — shellfish, squid, whole fish over charcoal with lemongrass and chilli. Eat at plastic stools facing the Thu Bồn River and watch the light go.

**An Hội, across the footbridge.** The locals' side of the river for beer and grilled skewers — cheaper than the Old Town and louder in a good way.

## How to eat well and not get sick

- **Plastic stools and a short menu are the signal.** A stall doing one dish properly beats a restaurant doing fifteen adequately.
- **Eat two or three bites per stop, across six or eight stalls.** This is how a food walk works here; you stay light and taste more.
- **Ice:** tube ice and ice from sealed bags is normal and generally safe. Be cautious with crushed ice in unsealed glasses on hot days.
- **Herbs and raw vegetables** are washed in the same water supply you should avoid drinking. If you have a sensitive stomach, ask for blanched vegetables.
- **Vegetarian food is everywhere.** Say *chay* (or *ăn chay*). Buddhist influence means most kitchens can produce a proper vegetarian version, not just a plate without meat.
- **Prices are fixed** in most stalls and all restaurants. Bargaining over a 30,000 VND bowl is not a local custom.

## Cooking classes worth the morning

A class is the fastest way to understand why the food tastes the way it does. The good ones start at the market, not in a kitchen.

- **Trà Quê village classes:** herb harvest, market tour, three dishes, lunch. Around $40–50 for a half day.
- **Red Bridge:** boat to the market, herb garden walk, four dishes. Long-running, well organised, book ahead.
- **Family kitchens in the Old Town:** two dishes, a real household, no stagecraft. Ask us to arrange one; they are not advertised.

Avoid any class that begins at 10 am in a restaurant with laminated menus and no market visit. You will learn to follow a recipe, not to cook.

## FAQ

### What is Hoi An's signature dish?
Cao lầu. It is the only dish in Vietnam that is genuinely tied to this town's water and technique, and it is what cooks here will tell you to eat first.

### How much should a meal cost?
Street food runs 20,000–60,000 VND per dish. A sit-down dinner with seafood and drinks is 250,000–500,000 VND per person.

### Is Hoi An street food safe?
Generally yes, at busy stalls with high turnover. Choose places where locals are eating, avoid pre-cut fruit sitting in the sun, and carry oral rehydration salts rather than skipping meals.

### When is the lantern festival?
On the 14th night of each lunar month, the Old Town turns off electric lighting and hangs lanterns only. It is crowded, beautiful, and worth planning your dates around.

### Can I do Hoi An in one day?
You can see the town in a day. You cannot eat it in a day. Two nights is the minimum for market mornings, a cooking class and a proper seafood dinner.
