# The Ha Giang Loop: four days on Vietnam's most demanding road

Roughly 350 km of mountain road, a limestone plateau old enough to hold fossils from half a billion years ago, and corners where the guardrail is a painted line and a drop. The Ha Giang Loop is not a scenic drive. It is a physical commitment, and it rewards people who prepare for it.

This guide covers the route, the three ways to ride it, the safety rules that actually matter, and the mistakes that turn a great four days into a miserable one.

## Before anything else: where you are going

> **Administrative note (2026):** on 1 July 2025 Ha Giang province was merged into Tuyen Quang. The destination is still universally called "Ha Giang" and the loop is unchanged, but the province on your paperwork and newer maps will read **Tuyen Quang**. Ha Giang city itself is now split into Ha Giang 1 and Ha Giang 2 wards.

The loop runs through the **Dong Van Karst Plateau UNESCO Global Geopark** — 2,356 km² of limestone, recognised in 2010 as Vietnam's first global geopark. The classic circuit goes clockwise: Ha Giang city → Quan Ba → Yen Minh → Dong Van → Meo Vac → Bao Lam → back to Ha Giang city.

## When to go

| Season | Months | What it looks like | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spring** | March – May | Plum and peach blossom, clear days | Occasional haze; some rain from late April |
| **Summer** | June – August | Flooded terraces like mirrors, waterfalls at full flow | Afternoon storms, landslides, the riskiest riding |
| **Autumn** | September – November | Golden rice around Hoang Su Phi, dry and stable | Busiest months; book beds early |
| **Winter** | December – February | Buckwheat flowers, clear cold skies, 5–15°C | Genuinely cold on a bike, especially at dawn |

**The best compromise for most riders:** late September to mid-November. The light is clean, the rice is gold, and the road is dry.

**The season to be honest about:** July and August. The scenery is at its most dramatic, but heavy rain brings landslides and slick clay, and a mountain road is a poor place to test your limits.

## Three ways to ride it

### Easy rider (a local driver, you sit behind)

This is what most first-timers should choose. You get the views and the photographs; someone who has ridden these corners a thousand times handles the road.

- **Who it suits:** solo travellers, first-time visitors to Vietnam, anyone who wants to look rather than steer.
- **What it costs:** a three-day, two-night package typically runs in the region of US$180–250 including driver, accommodation, most meals and permits. Four days costs more.
- **What to check:** that the driver has a proper licence, that a helmet is provided and fits, and that the price includes the border-area permit rather than adding it later.

### Self-drive

The version people imagine, and the one with real consequences.

- **Who it suits:** riders with genuine mountain experience — hairpins, livestock, fog, and roads that narrow without warning.
- **Rental:** roughly US$15–25 per day for a semi-automatic or manual bike.
- **Licensing:** Vietnam recognises the 1968 International Driving Permit. A home licence alone, or a 1949 IDP, does not legally cover you on a motorbike here — and an invalid licence can void your travel insurance precisely when you need it.
- **Insurance:** confirm in writing that your policy covers motorbike riding in Vietnam, including medical evacuation.

### Jeep or private car

Slower to feel, easier to survive.

- **Who it suits:** families, couples, anyone with mobility concerns, and anyone travelling in heavy rain.
- **Cost:** a four-day, three-night private vehicle with driver typically sits around US$350–450, depending on vehicle and group size.
- **Trade-off:** you lose the wind and the immediacy, but you gain luggage space, air conditioning and a driver who knows when to stop.

## The four-day route

### Day 1 — Ha Giang to Yen Minh

Leave Ha Giang city early and head north on QL4C. The jungle gives way to limestone within the first hour.

- **Quan Ba Heaven's Gate:** the first proper pass and the first view that makes people stop the bike. The twin hills below are the reason for the name.
- **Tam Son:** lunch town, and the last place with comfortable options before the road gets serious.
- **Yen Minh:** pine forest, cooler air, and a night in a Tay stilt-house homestay.

Riding: roughly 100–120 km, but mountain kilometres are not flat kilometres. Expect five to six hours with stops.

### Day 2 — Yen Minh to Dong Van, with Lung Cu

- **Nine Turn Pass (Doc Chin Khoanh):** nine hairpins dropping into the valley. Ride it slowly; it is a road, not a racetrack.
- **Lung Cu flag tower:** the northernmost point of the loop, with views across the border. The final climb is a long staircase — bring water.
- **Sa Phin:** the Vuong family palace, a hybrid of local and European architecture built in the early 20th century on opium wealth. Worth an hour.
- **Dong Van:** old town with clay-brick lanes, and a market that swells at weekends.

Riding: roughly 120–140 km with the detours.

### Day 3 — Dong Van to Meo Vac over Ma Pi Leng

This is the day people come for.

- **Ma Pi Leng Pass:** about 20 km of road cut into the cliff face, with the Nho Que river running through the gorge far below. Stop often. Every bend is a different photograph.
- **Nho Que river:** a detour down to the water, where boats and kayaks run through the gorge.
- **Meo Vac:** if your timing lands on a Sunday, the market here is one of the best in the north.
- **Bao Lam:** a quiet hamlet to finish the day, with warm hosts and very little else — which is the point.

Riding: roughly 130–150 km.

### Day 4 — Bao Lam back to Ha Giang

- **QL34 southwest:** gentler gradients, fewer passes, the Gam river valley opening out.
- **Bac Me:** a stop for pho and a stretch of the legs.
- **Ha Giang city by early afternoon:** then the six-hour bus or private transfer to Hanoi.

Riding: roughly 150–170 km.

**If you have a fifth day:** add Cao Bang and Ban Gioc waterfall, or return by a different route rather than retracing QL34.

## Safety: the part that decides the trip

1. **No riding after dark.** No guardrails, livestock on the road, and corners that appear without warning. Plan to be at your homestay before sunset.
2. **Ride your own pace.** Group rides break when someone tries to keep up. The fast riders will wait at the next junction.
3. **Brake before the corner, not in it.** Wet clay and loose gravel sit on the outside of bends.
4. **Watch for trucks and buses on blind corners.** They take the whole road and they are not slowing down.
5. **Helmet, always, properly fastened.** This is not a formality.
6. **Check the bike before you leave.** Brakes, tyres, lights, mirrors. Photograph the existing damage so you are not charged for it.
7. **Do not ride when exhausted.** Day two and day three are long; a fifteen-minute stop is cheaper than a mistake.
8. **Carry cash.** ATMs exist in Ha Giang city and Dong Van, and nowhere else reliable.

## Permits and paperwork

Parts of the loop pass through border areas, and a permit is required for some of them. Reputable operators include this in the package price and handle it before departure. If you are self-driving, ask explicitly where the permit is issued and which sections require it — do not assume the checkpoint will wave you through.

Bring your passport (not a photocopy) for checkpoints, and keep a photo of your insurance details on your phone.

## What to pack

- **Rain gear that works:** a proper poncho and dry bags, not a cheap plastic coat that shreds at 40 km/h.
- **Layers:** a base layer, a fleece, and a windproof shell. Temperatures swing between 10 and 30°C across a single day.
- **Footwear:** broken-in boots or grippy trainers. No flip-flops on a motorbike, ever.
- **Power:** a 20,000 mAh power bank. Electricity in homestays is intermittent.
- **Motion sickness tablets** if you are riding pillion.
- **Documents:** passport, visa, licence and IDP if self-driving, insurance printout.

## FAQ

**Do I need a licence to ride the loop?**
Yes. Vietnam recognises the 1968 IDP. Riding on a home licence alone is not legal and can invalidate your insurance.

**Is the loop safe for a solo female traveller?**
Many women ride it solo, usually as a pillion with an easy rider. Choose a licensed operator, share your location with someone at home, and trust your instinct about drivers.

**How fit do I need to be?**
Riding pillion is not physically demanding, but five to seven hours a day is tiring. Self-driving requires real stamina and concentration.

**Can I do it in three days?**
Yes, but you will spend most of it on the bike. Four days is the minimum for the loop to feel like travel rather than transport.

**What if it rains the whole time?**
Rain is manageable with proper gear, but landslides can close sections. Build one buffer day into your plan, especially in July and August.

**Is it suitable for children?**
As a pillion, generally not recommended under about twelve. A private car is the better family option.
